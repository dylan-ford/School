Module 9 | Self-esteem

Lay theories of self-esteem
- General assumptions are that high self-esteem makes us feel good and makes us feel positively about ourselves
- instead, Leary and colleagues argue self-esteem provides us with important information about our inclusionary status - the degree to which other people value us and want to include and accept us
The need to belong
- social inclusion and acceptance is vital for survival
- bonding with others results in greater chance of survival and greater reproductive success
- if a process was adaptive for out ancestors, corresponding genes were more likely to be passed on, becoming more common
- we have evolved to innately think, feel, and respond to others in ways that promote social bonding and inclusion
Self-esteem as a sociometer
- acts as a gauge to how others are valuing us; out social inclusion vs social rejection
- high self esteem = subjective appraisal that others are accepting them and valuing them; included as part of social groups and communities I belong to
- low self-esteem = subjective sense that they aren't reacting to them positively, that they aren't valued or accepted
	- which indicates we need to change something about ourselves
- sociometer operates continuously and responds to cues indicating social acceptance vs social rejection
- negative affect or low state self-esteem:
	- alert possibility of social exclusion/rejection
	- motivates action toward social inclusion
State vs trait self-esteem
- state self-esteem: monitors current relational value (inclusion vs exclusion in immediate context)
- trait self-esteem: assesses the degree to which one is the sort of person who generally will be valued by desirable groups and relationship partners
Trait self-esteem
- what most people think of when they think of self-esteem - indicate the degree to which i generally evaluate myself positively or negatively
- Out global or chronic level of self-worth - so in this sense it is more stable (doesn't change on environment)
- so trait self-esteem may be regarded as the resting state of the sociometer in the absence of incoming information relevant to relational value
- self-esteem was low during childhood increased throughout adolescence and young adulthood, and declined during midlife and old age
- Predictors of trait self-esteem
	- internal measure of one's potential for inclusion in desirable groups and relationships over the long run
	- built on experience (social feedback and acceptance)
	- based on whatever characteristics those important groups use to include or exclude individuals; competence, likeability, attractiveness, trustworthiness, general moral character
	- encompasses anticipation of interpersonal outcomes - a subjective projection of long-term relational value (will I be accepted in the future?)
	- quality of home environment in early childhood is a significant and lasting predictor of self-esteem
		- quality of parenting (warmth, responsiveness)
		- cognitive stimulation...
		- ...
	- a study of 241 students that reported quantity and quality of daily interactions
		- quality of interactions was associated with higher general levels of trait self-esteem
		- quantity of interactions not associated with trait self esteem
Impact of self-esteem
- thousands of studies have demonstrated that high self-esteem is associated with beneficial outcomes
	- happiness, life satisfaction
	- low-self esteem, however, is associated with undesirable outcomes
	- Debate in the literature about whether self-esteem causes these outcomes
- stronger evidence that low self-esteem does cause/is associated with :
	- dissatisfaction in marriage and close relationships
	- smaller social networks/support
	- worse health
	- lower education attainment
	- unemployment/job success and satisfaction
	- criminal behaviours
Perils of low self-esteem
- people become attuned to the degree to which they are being accepted or rejected
- Greater need for social approval, fear negative evaluation, socially anxious and rejection-sensitive
- lower self-esteem = more brain activation in brain areas associated with experiencing and regulating pain
