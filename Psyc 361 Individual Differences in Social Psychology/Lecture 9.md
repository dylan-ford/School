Module 8: Attachment Insecurities
- Two aspects of attachment theory
	- normative
		- deals with normal features of attachment behavioural system and its ideal development
	- Individual differences
		- differences in how the attachment system develops across individuals
- What is attachment?
	- Began by observing infant/caregiver relationships by Bowlby 1969, 1973, 1980
	- Theory grounded in evolutionary theory to describe a regulatory innate behavioural system
	- States that creatures are born with innate tendencies to seek proximity and safety from caregiver 
		- outlined evidence: eye tracking, clinging, smiling, crying
- Normative attachment processes
	- Evolutionary function of the attachment system:
		- To maintain proximity to young to caregivers for protection from environmental threats and increase changes of survival. How? by resisting and experiencing distress at separation to maintain closeness and protection by caregiver
		- Behavioural system is most activated when infant in danger or distress. The caregiver is a safe haven in times of distress - goal is to regulate emotional distress and restore felt security
		- When security is established, caregivers provide a secure base from which infants can explore the environment and cultivate autonomy
- From Bowlby to Ainsworth
	- Argued that normative process and attachment system is a good thing but normative processes reflect the ideal situation but not all relationships develop in an ideal manner
		- When caregivers don't provide safety and care, babies have to change the ways they adapt
	- Concerned with whether children were attached, but also how they are attached
- Individual differences
	- quality of attachment is determined by history of attachment interactions and responsiveness of attachment caregiver
	- Develop schemas for self and others (working model)
		- Beliefs and expectations about whether others will be responsive to your needs
		- Influences psychological processes and behaviours
	- Children develop schemas about beliefs and expectations about caregiver and whether or not they/people can be trusted - influences behaviour when distressed and when infant/child wants to explore world around them
- Responsive caregiver
	- Responsive = when caregiving behaviours address needs of the person
	- Perceived responsiveness: degree to which one believes/trusts attachment figures will be responsive to needs
		- This determines attachment security
- Strange situation
	- assessing infant attachment through separation and reunion with caregiver in a lab
	- secure: 60%, explores when CG is present, upset when CG leaves, easily soothed on return
		- sensitive and responsive caregiving = trust availability of love and support, seek proximity ad contact, and then use caregiver as secure base
	- anxious: 20%, clings to CG, extremely upset when CG leaves, upset (less) when CG returns
		- Receive inconsistent and erratic responsiveness = anxious about availability of love and support, inappropriate and prolonged protest, and do not accept comfort
	- avoidant: 20%, explores when CG is present, not as upset when CG leaves, distant/withdrawn when CG returns, plays with toys
		- perceive CG are consistently unavailable over time; unresponsive, rejecting and cold caregiving - cope through defensive avoidance of contact and support, independent and self-reliant, and cope by showing little emotion
- Impact of infant attachment over time
	- Bowlby argued that early life experiences shape peoples outcomes later in adulthood
	- Examining the impact of infant attachment on outcomes in adulthood is extremely difficult
	- only a few studies to date have examined these processes
	- infants rated as insecure continuously at 12 and 18 months report worse health-related problems at age 32
	- insecure infant attachment with mothers associated with:
		- difficulty judging emotion faces and mixed emotions in middle childhood
		- worse emotional and social development in early adulthood
		- negative emotions during relationship conflicts in adulthood
		- difficulty recovering from relationship conflict in adulthood
		- worse relationship quality during problem-solving in adulthood
	- Anxious and avoidant attachment patterns are also unique
		- have different developmental backgrounds, and they have different implications from how people regulate their emotions
- Impact of infant attachment on adult emotion regulation
	- Infants rated as stable insecure report greater hypo-regulation strategies during discussions with their romantic partners 19 – 35 years later
	- Infants rated as unstable insecure report greater hyper-regulation strategies during discussions with their romantic partners 19 – 35 years later
- Attachment in adulthood
	- similarities in primary attachment functions
		- proximity-seeking
		- separation protest
		- secure base
	- differences in infant and adult attachment
		- mutuality (care-seeking and care-giving)
		- psychological (not just physical) proximity
		- sexuality
- Attachment threats in adulthood
	- individual threat: when individual needs support, care, and protection
	- a close other experiences threat: SO, need to serve as the attachment figure
	- relationship threat: relationship conflict, undesirable partner actions such as the partner failing to provide necessary support, presence of attractive alternatives
- High attachment anxiety
	- background
		- inconsistent caregiving
		- tend to report conflict, enmeshed family backgrounds
	- defining features
		- low self-worth, believe they are unlovable
		- intense fears of abandonment and rejection
		- hypervigilant to potential signs of rejection
	- when distressed
		- excessive reliance on others, dependent and needy (jealous, possessive, etc.)
		- intense negative affect, insecurity and clinginess
		- demanding of closeness, attention, and approval
- High attachment avoidance
	- background
		- cold and rejecting caregivers
		- tend to report distant family relations
	- defining features
		- deep distrust of others' intentions and reliability
		- values self-reliance and independence
		- downplays importance of close relationships
		- rebuff intimacy and closeness
	- when distressed
		- avoid seeking support
		- suppress attachment needs/emotions/feelings
		- defensively avoid dependence and closeness
		- blame others and withdrawal/disengage
- Attachment across time
- Infant attachment to adult attachment
	- infant to adult attachment relatively stable
		- rs = .6
		- categorically stable; 60%
		- generally stability occurs when people experience few negative life events
	- changes rom secure to insecure do occur and are often associated with important life events
		- death of a loved one
		- parental divorce
		- physical or psychological illness
		- transition to parenthood
		- abuse or trauma
- Attachment operates as a flexible system
	- attachment insecurity can and does change based on experiences
	- expectations are additive - each relationship adds to, not replaces, our experiences and expectations
	- changes can also be "slow" and not the result of drastic life events
