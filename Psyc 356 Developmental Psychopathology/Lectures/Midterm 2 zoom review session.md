Chapter 5 Disorders of Early Childhood
- Developmental foundation
	- the first years of life represent a failure or success in the development self-regulation and interpersonal abilities for children
- Major development issues
	- Infancy: formation of an effective attachment
		- establishing basic state and arousal regulation, reciprocity, and the dyadic regulation of emotion
	- Toddlerhood: guided self-regulation
		- period characterized by emerging autonomy, self-awareness, and the initial internalizing of behavioural standards
	- Preschool: self-regulation
		- children move toward self-reliance with support (agency), expanding their social world and internalizing rules and values
- Disorder profiles
	- Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)
		- maladaptive eating patterns
		- Tied to sensory sensitivities or lack of interest in eating which disrupts early homeostatic regulation
	- Sleep-Wake Disorders
		- Disruptions in the maturation of sleep-state regulation
		- Viewed developmentally, early physiological dysregulation can serve as a precursor to broader regulatory failures
	- Differential diagnosis: Reactive attachment disorder (RAD) vs Disinhibited social engagement disorder (DSED)
		- Core clinical presentation
			- RAD: inhibited, emotionally withdrawn behaviour, child rarely seeks/responds to comfort
			- DSED: child actively approaches and interacts with unfamiliar adults impulsively
		- Affective characteristics
			- RAD: limited positive affect; episodes of unexplained irritability, sadness, or fearfulness
			- DSED: overly familiar verbal or physical behaviour; diminished or absent checking in with caregivers
		- Etiological context
			- RAD: rooted in a history of extreme insufficient care (neglect, deprivation)
			- DSED: often associated with social neglect or frequent changes in primary caregivers that prevent stable attachment
- Etiological factors: clinical perspectives - Romanian Orphanages Case Study:
	- children in these institutions lacked the "irreducible needs" or nurturing relationships and stable communities. Interventions demonstrated that while early neglect creates severe developmental risks, the move to stable, nurturing environments can initiate "self-righting" tendencies, though outcomes often depend on the duration and timing of the initial deprivation
Chapter 6 Intellectual Developmental Disorder and Learning Disorders
- Theories of intelligence:
	- cognitive development contains the master of age-appropriate tasks related to information processing and adaptive functioning
	- Zigler's developmental approach to IDD: viewed IDD not as a "broken" process, but as a developmental one. positing that individuals with IDD pass through the same stages of cognitive development as typically developing children, but at a slower rate and with a lower ceiling of attainment
- Genetic syndromes (neurobehavioural phenotypes)
	- Down syndrome: associated with the 21st chromosome, presenting with physical growth delays and a phenotype of mild to moderate intellectual disability
	- Williams syndrome: characterized by cardiovascular challenges and a unique personality phenotype ("cocktail party") which involves high sociability and verbal skill
	- Fragile X syndrome: the leading inherited cause of IDD; the phenotype frequently includes social anxiety, sensory sensitivities, and poor eye contact
	- Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder: resulting from prenatal alcohol exposure, this phenotype encompasses a range of cognitive impairments, in executive function and social judgement
- Learning disorders
	- distinct from IDD as they involve specific deficits in areas like reading or math rather than generalized impairment in intellectual or adaptive functioning
	- Developmental course: these disorders often surface during the school years as the task of competence becomes paramount
	- Role of the family: families are critical in providing scaffolding for adaptive functioning, advocating for inclusivity, and protecting the child's self-esteem from the stigma of academic struggle
Chapter 7 ASD
- Coordination of social-cognitive domains
	- ASD represents a failure in the co-construction of the human brain through social collaboration
		- Theory of Mind: the capacity to understand that others possess internal mental states. in ASD, the inability to mentalize creates a profound social-cognitive deficit
		- Affective social competence: Involves the coordination of emotional signaling during interaction, which is often atypical in ASD
- Clinical presentation
	- Social and communication deficits: impairments in social-emotional reciprocity and nonverbal communication
	- Restricted, repetitive behaviours/fixated interests: Stereotyped movements, insistence on sameness, and atypical sensory responses
- Case study example: Max and Aisha - from a physiological perspective, Max's busy intensity suggests a different regulatory failure than Aisha's withdrawal. In ASD, we look for "orchid-like" sensitivities. Max's "busyness" in a classroom might reflect sensory seeking, whereas Aisha's social worry might be exacerbated by a failure to read social cues (ToM)
- Etiology and trajectory
	- Etiology: a complex G x E x T interaction (gene, environment, time) involving risk alleles that impair general brain networking and connectivity
	- Trajectory: symptoms are detectable in infancy and persist into adulthood. Outcomes are highly dependent on early intervention and the child's level of intellectual functioning
- clinical perspectives: intervention models
	- TEACCH model: emphasizes structured teaching, utilizing visual supports and organized environments to accommodate the unique learning styles and processing difficulties inherent in ASD
Chapter 8 Maltreatment and Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders
- Stress and coping framework
	- early adversity "gets under the skin" by dysregulating the neuroendocrine and immune systems
	- Brain-body-behaviour: trauma impacts the immune system, the gut & metabolic system (energy conversion) and the neuroendocrine system (hormonal balance). Chronic stress leads to a biological embedding of the fight-or-flight response
- Disorder categories:
	- maltreatment: encompasses physical, sexual, and emotional abuse and the pervasive impact of neglect
	- Trauma-related disorders: PTSD, which arises from catastrophic events
	- Developmental Trauma Disorder: a proposed framework to describe the cascading consequences of chronic interpersonal trauma that current PTSD criteria often fail to capture
- Developmental Course
	- maltreatment leads to developmental cascades, where the failure of early attachment disrupts subsequent tasks of self-regulation and competence
	- there is a robust longitudinal link between early maltreatment and later depression, as biological systems become "primed" for negative affect and withdrawal
Chapter 9 ADHD
- Regulatory framework
	- ADHD represents a failure to master the challenges of self-regulation, effortful control, and executive function. It is the transition from the typical high energy of the "toddler period" to an atypical pattern of chronic impairment
- Diagnostic criteria and gender
	- core characteristics: inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity
	- sex/gender: males are diagnosed at higher rates, though females often present with more inattentive symptoms. Heritability of these traits increases as children age, reflecting the growth influence of genetic factors over time
- Etiology
	- Diathesis stress: a child may have a genetic predisposition (diathesis) for ADHD that only manifests as a disorder when triggered by environmental stressors like family conflict
	- Differential susceptibility (orchid dandelion): dandelion children remain resilient across environments. orchid children may be highly sensitive. in high-stress disorganized environments (like school) symptoms can escalate but in highly structured, nurturing environments they can flourish
- clinical perspectives: MTA cooperative group study
	- this study demonstrated that while pharmacological treatment is highly effective for reducing core ADHD symptoms, the combination of medication and psychosocial treatment leads to superior outcomes in social skills and academic performance
Chapter 10 Oppositional Defiant Disorder and Conduct Disorder
- foundations of prosociality
	- the school years require the development of empathy and cooperation. Bullying is viewed as a developmental failure in these prosocial systems, often rooted in early regulatory deficits
- Differential diagnosis
	- ODD presentation: a pattern of angry/irritability mood and argumentative/defiant behaviour towards authority
	- CD presentation: a more severe, repetitive pattern involving the violation of the basic rights of others or major societal norms (aggression towards people/animals)
	- Developmental course: ODD often appears in the preschool period. while not all children with ODD progress to CD, ODD serves as a common developmental precursor to the more severe antisocial behaviours seen in adolescence
- Etiology:
	- multifactorial: includes child factors (temperament), parent factors (discipline methods), peer factors (deviant groups), and sociocultural factors (neighbourhood stressors, gender roles)
- Clinical perspectives: prevention and intervention
	- the early risers program: a multicomponent intervention targeting at-risk children by involving the youth, parents, and school systems to prevent the development of chronic conduct problems
Role of risk and resilience:
- equifinality: different paths lead to the same outcomes. the genetic risk for ADHD and early brain injury can both result in the same clinical presentation of inattention
- multifinality: one cause leads to many different outcomes. As seen in the Romanian orphanages case, the same experience of early maltreatment can lead to RAD, DSED, depression, or even resilience

Lecture 4 Disorders of early childhood and intellectual developmental disorder
- Avoidant/Restrictive food intake disorder
	- ARFID is a feeding and eating disturbance characterized by significant nutritional or energy deficits
	- Based on clinical diagnostic criteria, it manifests across 3 primary dimensions:
		- Lack of interest: general lack of interest in food or the act of eating
		- Sensory sensitivity: avoidance based on the sensory characteristics of food
		- Fear of aversive consequences: anxiety or fear regarding the consequences of eating, such as choking or vomiting
		- (body image disturbance is not a prerequisite)
- Intellectual developmental disorder (IDD) framework
	- IDD is a developmental disability characterized by the significant limitations in both general intelligence (intellectual functioning) and adaptive functioning (conceptual, social, and practical skills)
	- Adaptive components
		- conceptual: difficulty learning new academic concepts; requires repeated instruction for reading, math, and problem-solving
		- social: difficulty making and keeping friends; easily persuaded or socially naïve, leading to being talked into trouble by classmates
		- practical: requires assistance with daily hygiene, lacks understanding of the practical application of money and time
- Zigler's developmental perspective
	- indicates children with IDD follow the same organized developmental sequence as typically developing peers. While the rate of change is significantly slower, the stages of development remain consistent and follow the same sequence
Lecture 5 ASD and ADHD
- ASD assessment: joint attention:
	- joint attention is a foundational social-communication construct. in the ADOS "bubble assessment," clinicians evaluate a child's ability to coordinate attention between an object and a person. The assessor blows bubbles, points at them, and uses and exciting vocalization to determine if the child will follow the point and share the experience through eye contact and gesture
- ADHD development tasks
	- core developmental challenges for children with ADHD involved the maturation of self-directed behaviours. students must distinguish between two primary challenges:
	- Executive functioning: ability to plan organize, and execute goal-directed activities
	- Self-regulation: the capacity for managing internal states and behaviours in response to environmental demands
Lecture 6 Maltreatment and trauma-related disorders
- neurobiology of stress
	- biological response to trauma involves a complex "top-down" and "bottom-up" interactions involving the amygdala, hippocampus, and frontal cortex
- Evolution of coping mechanisms
	- as children mature, their coping strategies transition from external reliance to internalized cognitive management
	- Developmental stage and primary behavioural strategy
		- Infancy
			- support seeking, proximity to caregivers, and co-regulation
		- young childhood
			- seeking safety and support from adults; physical escape from stressors
		- middle childhood
			- increase in complexity and number of strategies; transition toward cognitive strategies
		- adolescence
			- advanced problem-solving; improved emotion regulation; seeking peer support; matching specific strategies to the type of stressor
Lecture 7 Oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) and conduct disorder (CD)
- the foundation of prosociality
	- prosociality: voluntary behaviours intended to benefit others
	- conscience: an inner sense of right and wrong that guides behaviour
	- conscientiousness: a personality trait manifested through self-control and responsibility
- Prosocial motivations
	- other-focused: driven by empathy, sympathy, and principles of justice
	- self-focused (egoistic): driven by the desire to avoid negative emotions or personal punishment
- ODD diagnostic dimensions
	- clinicians must distinguish between the clinical profiling dimensions and the DSM-5 diagnostic categories
		- 4 clinical dimensions: oppositionality, irritability, aggression, and callous-unemotional traits
		- DSM-5 categories: angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behaviour, vindictiveness
- ODD developmental course
	- most children with ODD don't progress to CD, the progression pathway is the most problematic as it is associated with escalating violence and substance use
	- predictors of stability: early childhood onset, male gender, high initial symptoms severity, and negative parenting
	- predictors of progression to CD: male gender, lower SES, high levels of parental hostility
- CD specifics
	- involves a persistent pattern of violating the basic rights of others and major societal norms
	- limited prosocial emotions (LPE) specifier: child must persistently display 2 of the following traits over 12 months across multiple relationships and settings (25-35% of youth with CD display these traits):
		- 1. lack of remorse or guilt. doesn't feel bad after doing wrong; unconcerned about negative consequences
		- 2. Callousness: cold and uncaring, disregard for others, no empathy
		- 3. unconcerned about performance: lack of effort in school/work despite clear expectations
		- 4. shallow or deficient affect: insincere or superficial emotions, often used for manipulation
- Moffitt's developmental trajectories
	- life-course persistent trajectory
		- early childhood onset; stable externalizing seen in early school years
		- likely history of ODD and family history of antisocial behaviour
		- prognosis: worse outcomes; likely to persist into adulthood with violence
	- adolescence-limited trajectory
		- puberty onset
		- no significant conduct history prior to age 10
		- ends in young adulthood; less problematic long-term
- Etiology of conduct problems
	- genetic/neurobiological: polygenic in nature. Frick et al. (2014) found that genetic influence is significantly higher for those with CU traits (81%) compared to those with normative levels (30%). also involves low serotonin and HPA-axis/amygdala dysfunction
	- emotional/cognitive: high negative emotionality and hot executive functioning impairments; deficits in processing negative emotional stimuli
	- parental/family: harsh, inconsistent, or derisive (demeaning) parenting; attachment insecurity
	- peer/sociocultural: peer contagion; low SES stressors
- Sociocultural considerations and bias
	- diagnostic disparities are driven by systemic and clinician biases. African American boys are often perceived as older and less innocent, leading to the pathologization of their behaviour
		- African American boys being 2.5x more likely to receive a CD diagnosis than ADHD
		- 5x more likely than white children to receive and adjustment disorder diagnosis than ADHD
- Intervention strategies
	- child-level: CBT and cognitive problem-solving skills training to target information processing deficits
	- parent-level:
		- Parent management training: focus on contingency management (changing behaviour through consequences)
		- Parent-child interaction therapy: focused on the relationship/communication and the quality of interaction
	- system-focused: multisystemic therapy, an intensive approach addressing family, school, and legal systems
Developmental cascade framework:
- posits that early disruptive patterns are strongly associated with later, more complex difficulties
- these problems do not exist in isolation they reinforce one another across family, peer, academic, and health-related domains
- consequently, children do not simply "grow out" of these disorders without targeted, multicomponent intervention
