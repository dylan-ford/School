Developmental tasks and challenges related to the construction of self and identity
- Development of a coherent sense of self (characteristics, abilities, beliefs, values that compromise us) and positive identity (acceptance and prioritization of themselves, roles, responsibilities) are essential for healthy adjustment
- The construction of self is a process that starts in infancy and occurs within particular family, cultural, and environmental contexts
	- Adolescents are increasingly active agents in their own loves, therefore they have more influence on their development, experiences, and thus self-concept (selecting their environments)
- Another factor in development is one's ability to cope with stressors
	- valence of felt emotion can alter coping strategies and how one perceives themselves
- A key consideration in the development of self involves the degree of commitment to a particular experience or identity
	- Achievement and social respect contribute to positive self-esteem and rewarding relationships that serve as protective factors
- Self-knowledge is embedded in relationships and reflect others' views of oneself
- Narrative themes associated with well-being:
	- motivational themes - autonomy, connection
	- affective themes - emotional experiences
	- meaning making themes - exploration and evaluation of self and experience

Depressive disorders
- Major depressive disorder
	- characterized by sadness and a loss of pleasure accompanied by cognitive, behavioural, and somatic symptoms
	- Attention and concentration issues, rumination, thoughts of worthlessness and guilt
	- social withdrawal, fatigue, insomnia, changes in appetite/weight
	- Median duration of 4 months for children and 2 months for adolescents
- Persistent depressive disorder 
	- long standing disturbance of mood with ongoing sadness, irritability, lack of motivation
	- Less severe symptoms that MDD
	- Younger children often display more somatic difficulties and anxiety symptoms and struggle with externalizing behaviour
	- Adolescents struggle more with guilt and hopelessness, substance abuse, and suicidality
	- Both groups struggle with sleep disturbances
- Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder
	- Severe, recurrent temper tantrums that are atypical in intensity and frequency
	- Destructive tantrums, when present, are associated with later clinical disorders
	- Mood between temper outbursts is persistently irritable or angry most of the day
Bipolar disorders
- Severe forms of mood disorder involving alternating periods of depression and mania (increased energy, decreased need for sleep, increased risky behaviour, sometimes grandiose delusions)
- Hypomania: unusual and dysregulated emotions, thoughts, and behaviours similar to mania with no psychotic symptoms and less impairment
- Bipolar I: when an individual displays both depressive and at least 1 manic episode
- Bipolar II: when individual displays depressive and hypomanic episodes
- Cyclothymia: combination of hypomanic episodes and depressive symptoms that don't meet full BP diagnostic criteria
- BP development in children is rare and also not well understood

Developmental Course
- continuity of depressive disorders
	- struggles in childhood are associated with struggles in adolescence > adulthood 
	- Repeat episodes and worse outcomes are linked to sex/gender, early diagnosis, severity of depression, history of maltreatment, parent mental illness, and poor peer relationships (girls have more complicated clinical depressive presentations)
		- aware and involved parents and history of positive peer relationships are protective factors
	- Adolescents with depression have difficulties reconciling joy and success from their childhoods, making it harder to construct a sense of self that feels worthy and capable
	- young adults with a history of DMDD are more likely to struggle with depressive and anxiety disorders as well as interpersonal domains
	- Mood disorders can also be linked with special strengths like attenuation to others' feelings and sensitivities
- continuity of bipolar disorders
	- chronic pattern identified for children and adolescents
	- 25% of children with BP II convert to BP I over time
	- factors associated with worse outcomes: difficult temperament, rapid cycling of episodes, psychotic features, low SES, comorbidity, poor adherence to treatment

Etiology
- depressive disorders
	- depressed parent is one of the most powerful predictors of depression in children (likely to due environment and genetic factors)
	- atypical structure and function of brain regions and decreased blood flow to areas related to mood regulation are associated with emergence and maintenance of mood disorders
	- Young children with difficult temperaments, with increased irritability, and with negative emotions and decreased flexibility are at higher risk for the development of depressive disorders
		- depressed youth have worse sleep patterns, and worse sleep is associated with negative mood and risk-taking behaviour
	- social stress causes a permanent vulnerability in HPA axis - resulting in lower thresholds of stimulation to trigger an episode (kindling model - explains why subsequent depressive episodes occur from less stress)
- child factors
	- intense, prolonged, and poorly controlled experiences with emotion lead to the development of an affective bias
	- existing emotion-regulation strategies and perceived availability of emotion regulation strategies contributes to the development of depressive disorders
	- negative emotionality as a temperamental trait is associated with depressive disorders
	- emotional security is central to the regulation of many kinds of behaviour and is tied to:
		- child's emotional reactivity
		- representation of family relationships
		- regulation of exposure to family emotion
	- The hopelessness model emphasizes that negative inferences about the causes and consequences of events increase the risk for depression
- parent and family factors
	- caregiver depression increases the child's stress sensitivity and leads to the development of both internalizing and externalizing disorders
	- parent depression affects parent-child relationships and leads to child psychopathology
		- lower quality parenting
		- hinder secure attachment building
	- parent depression affects family relationships and causes family disruptions
	- parent depression affects relationships with romantic partners and that leads to child psychopathology
	- reinforcement model: depressed parents offer fewer rewards and more punishments to their children
	- high conflict has a disproportionate impact on adolescents girls
- environmental factors
	- genetically predisposed children are likely to be more susceptible to environmental factors like trauma and culture
	- negative life events and chronic hassles are associated with depression
- bipolar disorders
	- highly heritable
	- increased comorbid risk of other psychopathologies
	- brain regions involved in emotional and motivational processes are implicated in bipolar
	- reward hypersensitivity and social/circadian rhythm disruption
		- hyperactive motivational behavioural system; failure to achieve leads to depressive symptoms
		- disruptions in daily social rhythms lead to disruptions in circadian rhythms such as sleep patterns which lead to mood symptoms
Assessment and diagnosis
- children
	- agreement between parents and children about presence of depression is low
	- For disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, careful assessment involves close attention to atypical development in preschoolers and older children
	- hyperactivity and impulsivity are symptoms of ADHD and mania so bipolar can be hard to diagnose
		- presence of distinct episodes and appropriate developmental considerations are essential to accurate diagnoses
- adolescents
	- imperative to screen for suicidality
	- Diagnostic accuracy must also take into account differential diagnosis and comorbidity as well as developmental history and culture
Intervention
- motivation is crucial for treatment success
- most adolescents don't self-refer; reluctance
- immediate motivational work is needed to build therapeutic alliance to combat stigma and potential disadvantageous backgrounds
- youth prefer active psychological interventions like CBT
- treatment goals
	- manage acute symptoms
	- decrease distress and dysfunction
	- maintain improvements
	- reduce long-term complications
	- promote growth and development
- pharmacological treatments
	- antidepressants
		- most frequently prescribed for adolescents
		- no clear advantage over psychosocial interventions
- psychosocial treatments
	- CBT and interpersonal therapy
	- individual and group approaches are both effective
	- DBT effective for adolescent bipolar
	- sleep-targeted strategies often included in treatment as they are common to both depressive and bipolar disorders
- family treatments
	- parents can be powerful agents for change
	- psychoeducational support for parents
	- parent management training + individual youth treatment
	- address parental depression
	- marital therapy may help
Suicidality
- there are genetic effects on ideation, attempts, and death
	- higher concordance in monozygotic twins - disinhibition, impulsivity, aggression
- psychological factors
	- internalizing disorders: depression, bipolar, anxiety
	- externalizing disorders: ADHD, conduct disorders, substance use
	- hopelessness, guilt, shame
	- poor self-efficacy
- peer factors
	- social skills deficits
	- problematic peer relationships
- environmental factors
	- trauma, loss, maltreatment
	- failing academic performance
	- low SES, discrimination
	- access to lethal means
